Leaf-litter arthropods are the small jointed-legged animals that live in the layer of fallen leaves, twigs and loose soil on a forest floor. The layer stays cool, dark and damp, which makes it a refuge for animals that would dry out in open air. A single square metre of woodland floor can hold many hundreds of them, from spiders and centipedes to beetles, mites and springtails.[1]

All arthropods share a hard outer skeleton made largely of chitin, a body built from repeated segments, and legs that bend at joints. To grow, they must shed the old skeleton in a process called moulting, and they are soft and vulnerable for a short time afterwards.

Centipedes

Centipedes belong to the class Chilopoda. Their long, flattened bodies carry one pair of legs on each trunk segment, and the number of leg pairs is always odd, ranging from 15 to well over a hundred depending on the species.[2] The first pair of legs is not used for walking at all. It has become a pair of curved, venom-bearing claws called forcipules that grip and subdue prey.

Centipedes are fast predators of insects, spiders, worms and other small animals. Their outer skin lacks the waxy layer that keeps many insects from drying out, so they lose water quickly in dry air. For this reason they hide under stones, logs and leaves by day and come out to hunt at night.

When a centipede runs, its legs move in waves that travel along the body, so that neighbouring legs are always at slightly different points in their stride. The trick keeps the legs from tripping over one another and lets the animal change direction in an instant.

  • The head carries a single pair of long, sensitive antennae.
  • Most species have simple eyes or none at all, and hunt mainly by touch and smell.
  • The last pair of legs is often longer and points backwards, working more like a second pair of antennae.

Spiders

Spiders form the order Araneae, with more than fifty thousand described species. Every spider has eight legs and a body divided into two main parts, the front section that holds the legs and the rear section that holds the silk glands.[3] Silk leaves the body through small finger-like organs called spinnerets, and a single spider can make several kinds of silk for different jobs.

Many leaf-litter spiders never build a web. Wolf spiders chase their prey across the ground, and jumping spiders stalk it, using large forward-facing eyes that judge distance with remarkable precision before they leap.

A spider's legs work differently from our own. Muscles pull the joints inwards, but to stretch a leg out again the spider raises the pressure of the fluid inside its body. This is why a dead spider's legs curl up beneath it.

Moths and fireflies

Moths and butterflies together form the order Lepidoptera, and moths are by far the larger part of the group. Many night-flying moths have simple ears on the body that are tuned to the high-pitched calls of hunting bats. When a moth hears a bat closing in, it can drop, loop or dive out of the way.[4] Some tiger moths answer with ultrasonic clicks of their own.

Fireflies are not flies but beetles. Their light comes from a chemical reaction inside special organs on the abdomen, where a compound called luciferin reacts with oxygen with the help of an enzyme, luciferase. Almost none of the energy is lost as heat, which is why it is often called cold light. Each species flashes in its own rhythm, and the pattern helps males and females find one another in the dark.

Despite the name, no centipede has exactly one hundred legs, because the number of pairs is always odd. Millipedes are slow, rounded plant eaters that mostly feed on decaying leaves, and when threatened many of them roll into a tight coil rather than run.

Fossil history

Arthropods were among the first animals to live on land. Fossils show that centipede relatives were already hunting on land more than four hundred million years ago, long before the first trees.[5] During the Carboniferous period, a millipede relative called Arthropleura grew to well over two metres in length, the largest land arthropod known.

Today the leaf-litter community does quiet but vital work. By shredding fallen leaves and hunting the animals that do, these small creatures help return nutrients to the soil, keeping the forest floor alive from one season to the next.

Notes

  1. Numbers vary widely between forests, seasons and the depth sampled.
  2. The count of leg pairs in centipedes runs from 15 to 191.
  3. The front part is called the cephalothorax and the rear part the abdomen.
  4. Not all moths can hear. Hearing organs evolved separately in several moth families.
  5. Dates for early land animals come from fossils found in rocks of Silurian and Devonian age.
